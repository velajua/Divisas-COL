The peso bought time, not credibility

The market handed Colombia an unusual breather: on July 3 the dollar closed at COP 3,335.46, BanRep left policy rates at a very lucrative 12% floor, and the new fiscal message sounded far more austere than the recent script. The problem is that a currency can strengthen for a few days without the country solving the underlying issue: confidence still depends on whether promises turn into real discipline.

Analysis

Four signals behind the peso's latest relief rally

Between July 2 and July 5, several signals explained the recent enthusiasm in Colombia's currency market. But it helps to separate tactical price action from structural repair: a move in the exchange rate is one thing, a recovery in credibility is something else.

BanRep chose to defend its reputation
Monetary policy
The board hardened the signal Minutes published on July 3 confirmed that four board members voted to raise the policy rate by 75 basis points to 12%, while two preferred a cut and one wanted to hold. The takeaway is simple: with inflation still uncomfortable, BanRep did not want to look complacent.
That helped the peso, but through an expensive channel Such a high local yield makes Colombia attractive for carry-trade flows and temporarily supports the peso. Still, it remains a costly defense: the currency gains support because money is still expensive, not because sovereign risk has vanished.
The dollar fell hard, but markets also bought a story
Exchange rate
The benchmark returned to 2020 levels On July 3, according to Valora Analitik, the dollar fell another COP 25.59 from the previous close and ended at COP 3,335.46 after touching an intraday low of COP 3,323.01. That is not a trivial move: the market rewarded a mix of a softer global dollar and very high local rates.
Fiscal orthodoxy rhetoric also mattered In the same session, Colombia's designated finance minister spoke about meeting multilaterals, bondholders, and rating agencies, while also correcting excess spending and crowding out of the private sector. That more sober tone helped, but for now it is still an expectation, not a verified fiscal result.
Employment gave the positive narrative some oxygen
Real activity
The economy does not look collapsed DANE reported a national unemployment rate of 8.0% for May, down one percentage point from a year earlier, with the employment rate at 59.7%. That helps explain why the economy still appears able to absorb high interest rates without breaking immediately.
But a good labor print does not clear the fiscal front A firmer labor market can improve short-term sentiment, but it does not fix public-finance fragility on its own. The peso may welcome the figure; investors will still watch whether the state actually spends less and whether the promised tax reform arrives with numbers the market can defend.
Credibility is still on probation
Sovereign risk
BanRep itself described relief, not victory In the minutes, the board said country-risk premiums had fallen and that the move showed up in stronger local assets and a peso appreciation larger than that of comparable currencies. That explains what happened; it does not guarantee that markets already believe Colombia's adjustment is solved.
The next test is political and fiscal The Finance Ministry has already flagged a tax reform for July 20 during the transition process. If the new austere tone does not translate into spending cuts, credible financing, and less dependence on expensive local-currency debt, the peso's rally may end up looking like an elegant pause before another correction.

It would be a mistake to read the dollar's latest decline as a macroeconomic acquittal. What the market bought this week was a very specific tactical package: BanRep turned hawkish, the global dollar softened, and Treasury rhetoric started sounding closer to prudence than improvisation. That is enough to move prices. It is not enough yet to rebuild reputation.

In fact, the peso's recent strength carries an uncomfortable warning. If the currency needs a 12% policy rate to look solid, then support still depends on aggressive monetary medicine and on a pre-emptive vote of confidence in a fiscal correction that has not been executed yet. The market did not reward a reform already delivered; it rewarded the hope that someone may finally accept the need to deliver one.

The exchange-rate thesis for now is straightforward: Colombia gained time, not immunity. If the shift toward discipline is confirmed, the peso can hold a stronger range for longer. If improvisation returns or fiscal policy keeps promising more than it can pay for, the dollar will likely find room to regain ground rather quickly.
